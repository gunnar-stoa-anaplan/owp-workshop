OWP Lab Guide

Lab: Explore the CPP

A guided hands-on tour of the plan and its actions

CPP Lab Hands-On 30 min
📝 How this lab works

This is a guided exploration of your own OWP training tenant — there is no scripted dataset to follow step-by-step. Navigate your tenant's Continuous Position Plan (CPP), try each action described below, and observe what happens. Persona and number examples (Dorothy, Nisha, the $78k→$80k costing) are drawn from the workshop recordings so you know what to look for; your tenant's data will differ.

What you'll do

  • Navigate the position plan grid and read its columns — filled positions, worker names, org, cost center, costs, and location
  • Open a worker card and note the KPIs and the compa-ratio
  • Use "start anywhere, go everywhere" to open a job profile (skills, proficiency, critical-skill) and drill into an org
  • Try the create-position action and fine-tune a fully-loaded cost
  • Attempt a position transfer and a worker transfer, and set the backfill-vs-close flag
  • Run a skills match and tune the match threshold

1 — Navigate the position plan grid

The CPP is the dynamic, day-to-day view of the organization — hires, transfers, promotions, backfills, all linked to finance. Start by getting oriented in the grid.

In your tenant, open the Continuous Position Plan (CPP).

Find the position plan grid. Scan the columns and identify each one before touching any action.

  1. Identify which positions are filled versus planned, and read the worker names against the filled ones.
  2. Locate the columns for org, the associated cost center, the mappings, the costs, and the location.
  3. Find where each plan position was created — forecast versus budget.
  4. Notice the split between Plan actions (create a position, create many, edit in bulk, transfer positions, transfer workers, worker departures) and Analytics.
💡 Tip

OWP plans by position (filled / planned / open / closed) rather than by headcount. This position-level view is the best-practice approach the whole app is built on — the grid is where it becomes concrete.

Quick check

What is the APID (Anaplan ID), and what does it do?

Correct. The APID is "the thing that keeps all of this together" — assigned at position creation in OWP, written back into the HCM system as a field, and used as the key that follows the position through every system for the entire lifecycle (create → export to HCM → filled → actuals flow back).
Not quite. The right answer is C. The APID (Anaplan ID) is the code assigned when a position is created in OWP, written back into the HCM system, and it follows the position through every system for its whole lifecycle. Compa-ratio, performance rating, and budget-hierarchy mapping are separate concepts.

2 — Open a worker card and read the KPIs

Filled positions have a worker behind them. The worker card surfaces the KPIs the customer chose to expose.

In your tenant, click into a filled position to open its worker card.

On the recordings this was Dorothy (San Francisco) — in your tenant use whichever worker you land on.

  1. Note the standard KPIs: tenure, position, and location.
  2. Find the compensation breakout and the compa-ratio.
  3. Check whether a performance rating is shown (it appears only if that data was loaded).
  4. Notice the optional demographic KPIs (gender, generational distribution, race, ethnicity) — these are a per-customer DEI toggle, defaulted to "unlisted" or removed when the customer declines.

3 — Start anywhere: job profile and org drill

The links at the top of the CPP let you jump from any object to a related one — "start anywhere, go everywhere."

In your tenant, use the start-anywhere links to open a job profile, then drill into an org.

  1. Open a job-level detail page. Note the job level details, which orgs have this job, and the workers across the org who hold it.
  2. On the same page, find the required skills, the proficiency level, and whether each skill is flagged as a critical skill.
  3. Pivot to an org (on the recordings, the AR / accounts-receivable org). Note its filled positions, the workers and locations, and the org-chart widget.
  4. From the org, notice you can create a position or depart a worker without leaving the page.
📝 Why the org list is flat

The management / supervisory org list is kept flat because a person can live at an upper level and also in orgs below it. The hierarchy is reconstructed in the back end for aggregation and security — worth remembering when you configure selective access later.

4 — Create a position and fine-tune the cost

The CPP allows more precise, negotiation-aware costing than the budget side. Try the create-position action and adjust a fully-loaded cost.

In your tenant, open Plan actions and start "create a position."

Recording example: a sales rep with an out-of-the-box fully-loaded assumption of $78,000.

  1. Start a new position and note the out-of-the-box fully-loaded cost assumption ($78,000 in the example).
  2. Fine-tune it: add a 10% annual bonus and reduce base by $5,000 (to $75,000). In the example this yields a ~$7,300 annual bonus and the position created at ~$80,000 instead of the $78k baseline.
  3. Watch for guardrails via dynamic text rather than hard errors — e.g. "you don't have write access" or "your time to hire is too short for creating a position."
  4. Also look at create many positions and edit plan positions (bulk) in Plan actions.
📝 No approval queue for creating positions

There is no approval queue for creating positions — Workflow routes requests to the right person instead. A queue does exist when editing plan / requested positions.

Quick check

In the costing example, base is reduced to $75,000 and a 10% annual bonus is added, producing a position created at ~$80,000 instead of the $78k baseline. What is this meant to show?

Correct. The point of the $78k→$80k walkthrough is that the CPP lets you refine a fully-loaded cost — adjust base, add a bonus — to reflect the actual, negotiation-aware number rather than the coarser budget-side assumption.
Not quite. The right answer is B. The example shows the CPP enabling more precise, negotiation-aware costing than the budget side — the cost can go up or down, is not locked after creation, and the ~$7,300 bonus is derived from the 10% assumption, not a flat figure.

5 — Transfer a position and a worker

Transfers move positions or workers between orgs on a scheduled date. Try both, and pay attention to the backfill-vs-close decision.

In your tenant, try a position transfer, then a worker transfer.

Recording examples: Benjamin Rose (position transfer) and Yumiko, a senior hardware engineer in London (worker transfer).

  1. Position transfer: move a position from one org to another. Enter a reason and set a schedule date — note that transfers are scheduled at the month level, not day level. Observe the impact on both source and destination org.
  2. Try the cost-center override: transfer to an org but align the cost to a different cost center (the recording aligned to "Field Sales EMEA" instead of the default "outside sales").
  3. Worker transfer: search all planned/open positions filtered by a skill or title (the example filtered to "hardware") and pick a destination role for the worker.
  4. Set the backfill vs. close flag on the vacated position — it flips to open (backfill) or closed (close) on the scheduled date.
🚨 Transfer limitations

Transfers are one at a time — there is no mass transfer yet (a future enhancement). There is no approval process on transfers; a transfer simply executes when its date hits. Approvals on transfers are logged as a good future enhancement.

When you've navigated the grid, opened a worker card, drilled a job profile and an org, created a position, and run both a position and a worker transfer, click below to verify.

  • Worker card opened → tenure, position, location, and the compa-ratio noted
  • Start-anywhere used → a job profile (skills, proficiency, critical-skill) opened and an org drilled
  • Position created → fully-loaded cost fine-tuned ($78k→$80k example), dynamic-text guardrails observed

Next up: run a skills match and tune the threshold, then head to the debrief.

6 — Run a skills match and tune the threshold

Skills matching finds workers whose skills fit a role. Out of the box it is a manual process, and the threshold usually needs lowering.

In your tenant, run a skills match against a set of skills and adjust the match threshold.

Recording example: searching on accounts payable, financial analysis, financial modeling, and planning.

  1. Search workers by a set of skills and start with the match threshold at 75% — likely too high, returning too few results.
  2. Lower it to 50%, then to 25% — in the example, 25% gave the best results.
  3. Drill into a matched worker (the example used Jennifer Hills) to check role, tenure, and performance.
  4. Look at a job with a critical skill (the example was General Counsel with regulatory compliance) — this illustrates a future weighting module.
⚠ Skills data is dirty

Skills data is very dirty — "you have people that spell planning with four N's." That, plus matching being a manual process out of the box, is why the threshold usually has to be lowered to surface real matches.

Quick check

Why does the match threshold typically have to be lowered from 75% toward 25% to get useful results?

Correct. Because skills data is dirty (e.g. "planning" spelled with four N's) and matching is a manual out-of-the-box process, a strict 75% threshold surfaces too few candidates — lowering it toward 25% widens the net enough to find real matches.
Not quite. The right answer is A. The threshold is lowered because skills data is very dirty and matching is manual out of the box, so a high threshold returns too few results. It has nothing to do with cost, transfers, or the Optimizer, which is a separate (roadmap) extension.

Debrief Questions

  1. You opened a worker card that showed a compa-ratio but no performance rating. What does that tell you about the customer's data readiness, and how would you explain it?
  2. When you fine-tuned the fully-loaded cost from $78k to $80k, what did the CPP let you do that the budget side does not? When would a customer want this?
  3. On a worker transfer you set the vacated position to backfill (open) rather than close. Walk through what happens on the scheduled date for each choice, and who cares about the difference.
  4. Your skills match returned nothing at 75% and good results at 25%. What is really going on, and what would you tell a customer who expects clean matching out of the box?
