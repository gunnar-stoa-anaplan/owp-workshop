Exercise Overview

Skills matching helps you find internal candidates for a role by comparing worker skills against the skills a job needs. In this lab you'll search on a set of skills — for example accounts payable, financial analysis, financial modeling, and planning — and use the match threshold to widen or narrow the field. You'll see why a high threshold can return too few people, and why the data behind matching needs a careful eye.

💡 How to use this lab

Change one thing at a time. Lower the % match threshold in steps and re-read the results before you touch anything else — the whole point of the lab is to feel how the threshold trades precision for reach. Then drill into a promising worker to confirm role, tenure, and performance before you shortlist them.

Guided Steps — Search by Skill

Navigate to the CPP and open Worker Skill Search.

Skills matching is a manual process out of the box, so you drive the search: choose the category, mark the skills that matter, and set the threshold yourself.

  1. Open Worker Skill Search and pick a skill category.
  2. Mark the required and critical skills you're matching on — for example accounts payable, financial analysis, financial modeling, and planning.
  3. Set the % match threshold to 75% and read the results. At this level the bar is often too high and you'll get very few workers back.
  4. Lower the threshold to 50% and re-read the results — more workers qualify.
  5. Lower it again to 25%. In the worked example this returns the best working set of candidates to review.
  6. Review the Worker / Position / Job results and their match percentages. Drill to a candidate — for example Jennifer Hills — to check role, tenure, and performance.
  7. Shortlist an internal candidate that fits the role.

Quick check

You start at a 75% match threshold and get almost no workers back. Why lower it to 50% and then 25%?

Correct. The threshold sets how close a worker's skills must be to the job's skills. At 75% the bar is often too high and few people clear it; dropping to 50% and then 25% widens the field so you have a real set of candidates to review. In the worked example, 25% gave the best working set.

Quick check

A worker who clearly has planning experience isn't matching. What's the most likely cause, given how skills matching works out of the box?

Correct. Skills data is notoriously dirty — people spell "planning" with four n's, and the same skill shows up under different labels. Because matching is a manual process out of the box, an inconsistently spelled skill simply won't match, so you review and clean the underlying data rather than trusting the results blindly.
Not quite. The right answer is C. Matching is manual out of the box and skills data is dirty — inconsistent spellings and labels break the match. The Optimizer, selective access, and the threshold floor aren't what's dropping a genuinely skilled worker here.

Debrief Questions

  1. At 25% the results were the most useful, but a 25% match is a loose fit. How would you talent-review that list rather than treating the top match percentage as the answer?
  2. Skills data is dirty and matching is manual out of the box. What would you put in front of a customer as the data-cleanup work needed before they rely on skills matching?
  3. The General Counsel example flags regulatory compliance as a critical skill to motivate a future weighting module. How would weighting critical or expert skills change the candidate list you built here — and how would you caveat that as roadmap?
  4. You shortlisted an internal candidate. How does this feed a worker transfer — and what does it tell you about OWP as a foundation for finding talent you already have?
